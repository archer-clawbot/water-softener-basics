Water Softener Basics: A Beginner's Guide

Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. Over time those minerals can leave scale in pipes, water heaters and fixtures, spots on dishes, and soap that doesn't lather well. A water softener is one way to deal with it, but it isn't the right answer for every home or every water problem.

Start with a water test

Before buying any equipment, find out what's actually in your water. City water customers can ask their utility for its annual water quality report. Private well owners should test, ideally through a certified lab, because hardness often shows up alongside iron, manganese or other issues that a softener alone won't solve. If you're not sure softening is the right fix, this guide to choosing a home water treatment system walks through matching the treatment to the problem.

How a salt-based softener works

Most conventional softeners use ion exchange. Water passes through a tank of resin beads that hold sodium (or potassium) ions; the beads capture calcium and magnesium and release sodium in their place. Periodically the unit regenerates, flushing the resin with brine from a salt tank and sending the captured minerals down the drain.

What about salt-free systems?

Products sold as "salt-free softeners" generally don't remove hardness minerals. Many are conditioners meant to change how minerals behave so they're less likely to form scale. Whether that meets your goals depends on what you want to fix, so ask any seller exactly what the product removes and how that claim was tested.

Sizing matters

A softener needs enough capacity for your household's water use and hardness level. Too small and it regenerates constantly; too large and the resin may not regenerate efficiently. The water softener sizing calculator on Water System Guide gives you a starting point to bring to a dealer conversation.
